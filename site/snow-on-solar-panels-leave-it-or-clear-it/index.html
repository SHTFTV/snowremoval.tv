Snow on Solar Panels: Leave It or Clear It?

November 27, 2026 · SnowRemoval.TV Editorial

For most Lower Mainland homes, the best thing to do with snow on solar panels is leave it. Our snowfalls are usually short-lived, tilted panels shed snow as it softens, and a few days of lost production in December costs little compared with a damaged panel, a voided warranty or a fall from a roof. Clearing can make sense when the array is ground-mounted or within reach of a ground-level rake, when heavy wet snow is sitting on a low-slope array for days, or when snow sliding off the panels is threatening a walkway or entrance. Never climb onto a snowy roof to do it.

Why snow usually isn't a big deal here

Solar output in winter is low anyway. BC Hydro's guide to switching to solar says a typical residential system in BC is about 7 kW with 16 panels and generates around 7,700 kWh over a year, and most of that comes in the longer, brighter months. A short stretch of snow cover in late fall or winter removes a small slice of an already small share.

Panels also help themselves. The glass is smooth, most residential arrays are tilted, and once the sun or rain softens the bottom layer, the snow tends to slide off in sheets. The U.S. Department of Energy's guidance on hardening solar systems for winter notes that steeper tilt angles shed snow faster.

What can go wrong if you clear them yourself

Scratched or cracked glass. BC Hydro reminds homeowners that solar panels are essentially made of glass and need to be handled with care. Metal shovels, ice choppers and stiff brushes can scratch the surface or chip edges.

Warranty problems. Panel and installer warranties often spell out what owners may and may not do. HomeServe's guidance is to check the fine print before using any tool, because some cleaning methods can void the warranty.

Thermal shock. Pouring hot water on cold glass is a bad idea for the same reason it's a bad idea on a frozen windshield.

Falls. This is the big one. WorkSafeBC requires workers to use fall protection when they could fall 3 metres or more, or less where the landing is especially dangerous. Homeowners aren't covered by that regulation on their own roofs, but the physics are the same, and snow on top of slick glass is about as slippery as a surface gets.

Avalanches onto people. Panels shed snow suddenly. If your array sits above a door, path or driveway, a whole sheet can let go at once.

When clearing does make sense

  • Ground-mounted arrays. If you can reach the panels while standing on the ground, gently pushing snow off with a soft foam roof rake or a soft brush is low risk.
  • Persistent heavy snow on low-slope panels. Flat or shallow-angled arrays on carports and garages don't shed as well. If wet snow sits for days, light, careful removal with a long-handled rake can help.
  • Snow load concerns. The DOE notes frameless panels may have lower snow load ratings than framed ones. If you have an unusual setup, ask your installer what it's rated for.
  • Safety below the array. If snow slides could land on a walkway, clearing the path area or roping it off matters more than the panels themselves.

How to clear panels safely from the ground

  1. Check your warranty and installer's instructions first.
  2. Use a soft tool. A roof rake with a foam or soft-bristle head and a telescoping pole lets you work from the ground. HomeServe recommends never climbing a ladder in snowy conditions to reach rooftop panels.
  3. Pull gently, from the bottom edge. Don't scrape down to the glass. A thin layer left behind will melt quickly once the surface is exposed to light.
  4. Stand clear of the drop zone. Work from the side so snow doesn't land on you.
  5. Leave ice alone. If it's frozen to the glass, wait. Chipping at ice is how panels get damaged.
  6. Keep the area in front clear on ground-mounted systems so shed snow doesn't pile up and drift back onto the bottom row.

Leave it or clear it: quick guide

Situation Recommendation
Light snow, steep roof array Leave it
Heavy snow, steep roof array, nothing below Leave it
Roof array above an entrance or path Leave panels; manage the area below
Ground-mounted array Clear gently with a soft rake if you like
Low-slope carport array, heavy wet snow lasting days Consider careful removal at ground level or call your installer
Ice bonded to the glass Leave it
Any situation needing a ladder or roof access Don't do it yourself

If you hire someone

If snow is a real concern, call your solar installer first. They know the system, the mounting and what the warranty allows. A snow removal company working on a roof should have trained staff, fall protection and insurance, and should agree in writing to use soft tools that won't scratch the glass. For a sense of what else rooftop snow can mean for your home, see our earlier guides on roof rakes and when to hire a pro for roof snow.

The bottom line

Snow on panels feels like wasted electricity, but in the Lower Mainland it rarely stays long enough to matter. Let the sun and the slope do the work, protect the areas below, and only clear what you can safely reach from the ground. Your panels, your warranty and your back will all be better for it.

Frequently asked questions

Do solar panels work with snow on them?

A light dusting may let some light through, but covered panels produce little or nothing until the snow slides or melts off.

Will clearing snow void my solar panel warranty?

It can, depending on the tool and method. HomeServe advises checking your warranty before using any cleaning tool, and BC Hydro reminds owners that panels are glass and need careful handling.

Is it safe to use a roof rake on solar panels?

Only a soft foam or soft-bristle rake used gently from the ground. Avoid metal tools, scraping down to the glass, or chipping ice.

Can heavy snow damage solar panels?

Panels and racking are designed for snow loads, but the U.S. Department of Energy notes frameless modules may have lower snow load ratings. Ask your installer what your system is rated for.
