Snow on Solar Panels: Leave It or Clear It?

November 27, 2026 · SnowRemoval.TV Editorial

For a homeowner, the sensible starting point is to leave snow-covered solar panels alone while checking the system's winter maintenance plan. Do not climb onto a snowy roof or assume a long-handled rake makes the job safe. If there is concern about loading, damaged equipment or snow falling onto an entrance, contact the solar installer or a qualified solar service provider and protect the area below. Whether removal is needed depends on the actual array, roof and conditions.

Will the panels shed snow on their own?

They may, but roof slope alone cannot settle the decision. The U.S. Department of Energy's winter-weather guidance explains that tilt and module construction affect shedding. It also warns that snow can accumulate unevenly at the lower edge of a module and create additional stress. A steep array is not a guarantee that heavy accumulation is harmless.

For a Lower Mainland property, use the installed system's monitoring history and the installer's advice when weighing a production interruption. This article does not estimate your lost electricity, predict how long snow will remain or set a safe snow depth. Those answers vary with the equipment and site.

What can go wrong if you clear them yourself?

  • Electrical hazards. Solar panels are electrical equipment. Canadian Solar's installation manual, sections 7 and 8, warns that cleaning can increase electric-shock risk, especially with wet or damaged modules. It directs electrical and mechanical inspection and maintenance to qualified personnel. Standing on the ground does not remove those hazards.
  • Panel damage and warranty consequences. The same manual specifies a soft brush for snow and warns that inappropriate cleaning can damage modules and affect warranty coverage. That is product-specific guidance, not approval for every brush or rake on every brand. Obtain the instructions for your exact modules and mounting system.
  • Falls. WorkSafeBC's fall-protection guidance emphasizes planning, training and appropriate protection for workers exposed to fall hazards. A homeowner should not treat roof access as a routine cleaning task. Do not use a ladder or walk on the array to recover generation.
  • Falling snow and ice. Keep people away from areas where snow could shed. Do not stand beneath an array while trying to dislodge an accumulation.

When should you ask for an assessment?

Call the solar installer when the maintenance instructions are unclear, when snow loading is a concern, when the system appears damaged, or when the usual access route lies beneath accumulated snow. For a roof-capacity question, ask who can assess the roof structure as well as the modules and racking. A panel rating alone does not establish the capacity of the whole roof.

Provide the system make and model, any installation records, the location of the affected array and photographs taken from a safe position. Explain what has changed and where people normally walk. Do not approach damaged wiring or touch loose components to collect information.

The DOE generally discourages manual snow or ice removal from panels because it can cause damage. If exceptional accumulation requires intervention, the decision and method should come from the system-specific assessment. Ground-mounted panels and low carport arrays still need that assessment; reachable does not mean safe to clean.

What can you do without touching the panels?

  • Check the written winter plan. Find the installer contact, equipment manuals and any agreed inspection process before arranging work.
  • Manage access below. Keep people outside a suspected shedding area and arrange an alternative route where necessary. Coordinate with the property manager so emergency access and accessible entrances remain usable.
  • Tell the ground-clearing crew where the array is. Identify ground-mounted panels and the agreed snow-storage areas on the site plan. Keep routine plowing and stored snow clear of equipment.
  • Request advice about bonded ice. Do not chip, scrape or try to melt it with improvised tools or chemicals. Let the qualified provider determine whether intervention is required.

If you hire someone

Start with your solar installer. Ask the proposed provider to identify the equipment, confirm the manufacturer's maintenance method, explain how electrical and falling-object hazards will be managed, and define the work in writing. For roof work, ask about relevant training, insurance and applicable WorkSafeBC arrangements. Owning snow-removal equipment does not establish solar-maintenance expertise.

Keep rooftop solar work separate from the ordinary sidewalk and parking-lot service scope. This article does not offer solar-panel cleaning through SnowRemoval.TV or PlowWow. For related planning, read when to hire a professional for roof snow and snow storage and drainage.

Frequently asked questions

Do solar panels work with snow on them?

Snow cover can reduce production. Check the system monitoring and ask your installer about the actual array; a loss of generation by itself is not a reason to attempt unsafe clearing.

Will clearing snow void my solar panel warranty?

Coverage depends on the exact product and method. Canadian Solar warns that inappropriate cleaning can damage its modules and affect warranty coverage. Follow your own manufacturer instructions and confirm the maintenance plan with the installer.

Is it safe to use a roof rake on solar panels?

Do not assume so. Ground-level reach does not remove electrical, falling-snow or damage risks. A qualified provider should determine whether removal is necessary and which method is authorized for the installed system.

Can heavy snow damage solar panels?

Heavy or uneven loads can damage an array. Ask about the modules, racking and roof together; slope, a generic panel rating or the number of snowy days cannot establish safety.
